Sol's original Roblox game plan

3 INCHES TALL

A co-op open-world backyard survival/adventure game.

You wake up three inches tall in a gigantic suburban backyard. Build a vehicle out of junk, explore enormous everyday environments with friends, survive spiders, sprinklers, lawnmowers, dogs, storms and other backyard disasters, recover valuable salvage, and bring it back to your tiny settlement.

Grounded-size fantasy + Dead Rails-style expeditions + open-world driving + Brookhaven-style self-expression + collectible discovery + dynamic disaster events.

Original plan written September 2026. This page preserves the original concept and structure in a cleaner HTML format.

Part I — What Roblox actually looks like in 2026

The important lesson from the 2025 viral wave is that peak CCU is not the same thing as a durable business. Roblox's own direction has increasingly favored experiences with stronger long-term retention rather than pure short-term monetization.

The target therefore cannot simply be “go viral.” It needs:

player-generated situations identity / collection expandable progression cheap-to-produce updates co-play strong first-session comprehension

What discovery cares about

SignalWhy it matters
Play-through rateDoes the thumbnail/title actually make people enter?
<60 sec bounceDid they immediately decide the game was not for them?
61–180 sec bounceDid onboarding kill them?
D1 / D2–7 / D8–28 play daysDoes the game become a habit?
Playtime per userIs the game actually engaging?
Intentional co-playAre people deliberately returning with friends?
Qualified sessionsAre sessions substantive rather than accidental clicks?
Spend days / Robux per userDoes the economy create repeat value without sabotaging retention?
We are not optimizing for “How can we get 30,000 players on launch day?” We are optimizing for “How do we make the first 300 players come back so reliably that Roblox gives us 3,000, then 30,000?”

The spike-vs-durability lesson

Games can hit enormous peaks and then settle dramatically lower. The more useful long-term comparison is not “who had the biggest weekend?” but “who still matters years later?” Brookhaven and Blox Fruits are more strategically instructive than a one-week phenomenon because they created worlds people keep inhabiting.

The durable stack is:

Hook → Fun → Progression → Identity

Hook without depth creates a viral collapse. Depth without a hook gets ignored. Progression without fun becomes a treadmill. Fun without identity often gets completed and abandoned.

Why 3 Inches Tall still won

Shopkeeper Street actually produced the largest raw score, but I would not greenlight it first because its catastrophic failure modes include player-to-player economies, inflation, duping, marketplace exploits, moderation, AFK economies and economy design. One exploit can annihilate months of balancing.

3 Inches Tall had the best combination of ceiling and survivable execution risk.

Shopkeeper Street

Every player owns a store inside a living market district. Huge social upside; huge economy/exploit burden.

Heist Architect

Players build secure vaults and raid other player-designed layouts. Brilliant UGC loop, but hard backend/geometry/security problems.

Tiny Kingdoms

Build miniature kingdoms, visit, defend, cooperate. Fantastic identity but dangerously broad scope.

Creature Field Guide

Track, photograph, document and collect wildlife. Cheap updates, but must avoid becoming Pet Simulator with a nature skin.

Museum Hunters

Go on expeditions, recover artifacts, build a museum. Strong action-to-identity loop; slightly weaker immediate hook.

Junkyard Kings

Find wrecked cars, repair, customize, collect or flip them. One of the safest alternatives if vehicle modeling is a strength.

Stormline

Co-op storm chasing and rescue. Extremely shareable; weather and mobile performance are scary.

Island Rescue

Fires, floods, landslides, storms, boats, ATVs, helicopters. Great emergent gameplay but scope can explode.

Arcade Sports Club

One polished weird sport plus seasonal modes. Competitive retention, but needs great netcode and matchmaking.

Courier Chaos

Ridiculous packages, physics disasters, tiny vehicles. Great YouTube game, weaker D30 unless the depot/vehicle meta gets deeper.

Deep Salvage

Explore shipwrecks and submerged ruins. Good mystery/extraction fantasy; underwater controls are a mobile risk.

City Services

Tow trucks, electricians, firefighters, road crews and EMS in a persistent city. Could live forever, but massive production scope.

Expedition 13

Co-op roguelite archaeological expeditions with permanent base progression. Strong depth, weaker “one picture explains it” hook.

Night Shift

Run a gas station while increasingly weird things happen. Great first-video concept; surprise burns out.

Part III — The actual game

Core fantasy

Normal Roblox avatar. Normal suburban backyard. Except your character is approximately three inches tall.

A fallen hot dog is enormous. Grass reaches above your head. A soda can is shelter. A garden hose is a pipeline. A patio table is a skyscraper. A swimming pool is an ocean. A dog is effectively Godzilla.

And four players driving a vehicle made from toy wheels and AA batteries through all of it is inherently funny.

Ordinary object + enormous scale = content.

Elevator pitch

3 INCHES TALL is a 1–8 player cooperative adventure where players build junk vehicles, take expeditions into a giant backyard, survive dynamic environmental disasters, collect salvage and strange discoveries, and turn their tiny shed settlement into a thriving miniature town.

Target natural session length: roughly 12–25 minutes, with players free to stay longer.

The core loop

  1. Enter camp. Workbench, garage, map, trophy wall, contract board, other players, your vehicle.
  2. Pick an expedition. Battery Run, Missing Scout, Garden Research, etc.
  3. Travel. Walk or drive your junk buggy.
  4. Something changes. Sprinkler turns on, ant migration crosses the route, rain starts, dog gets released, debris falls.
  5. Make a risk/reward decision. Extract now or get greedy?
  6. Return. Loot becomes parts, settlement progress, vehicle mods, blueprints and collections.
  7. Show it off. Rare discoveries physically appear in camp.
  8. One more run.

The first 10 minutes

TimeExperience
0:00–0:15Spawn. Camera sells giant scale. Text: YOU'RE 3 INCHES TALL.
0:15–0:45Grab a bottle cap.
0:45–1:20Attach it to the damaged starter buggy using a highlighted socket.
1:20–2:00Garage opens. GO GET THE BATTERY.
2:00–5:00First loot, first bug, first hazard, first funny failure.
5:00–10:00Reach meaningful objective and return to permanent progression.

No five-minute tutorial. Everything after the first interaction is contextual.

Zone 1 — Shed Camp

Safe, warm, visually cluttered with trophies. Major stations: garage, workbench, contract board, radio, map, collection book, trophy shelves, tiny garden, wardrobe. Other players' vehicles stay visible to create aspiration.

Zone 2 — Patio Plains

Starter zone. Plastic forks, fries, grill, flip-flops, chair legs, soda cans, bottle caps. Enemies: ants, gnats, beetles. Hazards: human footsteps, broom sweep, grill heat.

Zone 3 — Garden Beds

Dense vegetation, wire, seeds, plant fibers, pollen and ceramic fragments. Sprinkler cycles transform paths into rivers and create temporary routes.

Zone 4 — Sandbox Dunes

Toy parts, buried treasure, ant tunnels. Soft terrain makes wheel selection matter: sand wheels, patio wheels, cheap bottle-cap wheels. Upgrades change decisions instead of just adding +12 damage.

Zone 5 — Pool Deck

The ocean biome. Pool noodles become islands, toys become structures, drain creates suction events. Boats are a future expansion, not launch scope.

Zone 6 — Compost Wilds

Rare biological materials, strange insects, poison/gas pockets, rotten fruit, huge mushrooms. Higher rewards and higher danger.

Zone 7 — Woodpile / Spider Hollow

Launch endgame zone. Vertical traversal, webs, darkness, spiders, rare fibers and nails. Spiders behave like horror predators, not MMORPG health bars.

Zone 8 — Dog Run

You hear the collar before seeing the dog. Players hide under objects, distract it, flee by vehicle or escape into pipes. The dog is not a boss to farm; it is weather with teeth.

Expansion roadmap

  1. Garage interior
  2. Front yard
  3. Storm drain
  4. Inside the house / basement
  5. Neighbor's yard
  6. Street

Part VI — Progression without simulator cancer

Use multiple meaningful progression tracks rather than a single infinitely inflating currency.

Scrap

Common soft currency for ordinary crafting/upgrades. Not directly sold at first.

Components

Physical loot: battery cells, springs, rubber, wire, bearings, plastic, cloth fiber.

Blueprints

Knowledge progression. Best items require discovery, not just currency.

Scout Rank

Account progression unlocking contracts and zones, not endless raw combat multipliers.

Vehicle Mastery

Using vehicle families unlocks customization and sidegrades.

Field Guide

Discover bugs, plants, weather, landmarks and strange objects. Huge completionist value.

Settlement

The camp visibly grows from cardboard shelters into a miniature town.

Personal Trophies

Rare discoveries become physical props in camp.

Gear philosophy

No Sword I → Sword II → Sword III treadmill.

  • Sewing Needle — fast melee
  • Thumbtack Spear — reach
  • Rubber Band Launcher — ranged
  • Matchbox Shield — defense
  • Magnet — pulls metal salvage
  • Suction Cup — traversal
  • Paperclip Grapple — vertical mobility

Vehicles

Vehicles are one of the strongest identity and monetization layers, but launch uses socketed construction instead of full Scrap Mechanic-style freeform building.

  • Chassis
  • Front wheels
  • Rear wheels
  • Storage
  • Power source
  • Utility
  • Cosmetic accessory

Examples: AA-powered buggy, wind-up toy chassis, skateboard truck, soapbox racer, pull-back vehicle.

Death

No “pay 49 Robux or lose 42 minutes” bullshit. Players get downed, friends can revive, full party wipes lose some expedition cargo, but permanent blueprints/discoveries are safe. Recovery bags can create a natural follow-up expedition.

PvP and trading

No launch PvP. It adds balance, cheating, toxicity and mobile/PC fairness problems without improving the core fantasy.

No launch trading. Trading requires dupe protection, provenance, rollback infrastructure, scam prevention and moderation. It can wait.

Part VII — Social systems

Social play should change gameplay instead of merely applying a +30% XP buff.

Expeditions

Solo viable. Two players noticeably more fun. Four players chaotic. Six-to-eight possible. No mandatory healer/tank/DPS roles.

Ping system

Mobile-friendly pings: Danger, Loot, Come Here, Help, Vehicle, Objective.

Haul table

At expedition end, recovered items physically dump onto a table. Instead of “+68 scrap,” players get a visual “LOOK AT ALL THE SHIT WE BROUGHT HOME.”

Group interaction

  • Some heavy objects need multiple people.
  • Different utility gear synergizes.
  • Multi-seat vehicles.
  • Group contracts.
  • Rescue mechanics.

Scout Club

Possible subscription value: exclusive cosmetic set, special paint, nameplate, extra outfit preset, extra trophy layout and monthly cosmetic currency.

Not: 2× damage, 2× materials, huge inventory advantages or the best weapon.

No paid lootboxes at launch

Icon

Tiny avatar on a bottle cap next to a giant sneaker or grass blade. The size disparity must read at phone-icon scale.

Launch stages

Phase 0 — Kill the idea with pictures

Create fake thumbnails and 10–20 seconds of representative footage. Ask target players what they think they do in the game. If they cannot explain “tiny backyard survival/exploration,” packaging failed.

Phase 1 — Ugly prototype

One hub, Patio, Garden, one buggy, one enemy, one sprinkler event, one expedition, one upgrade. No monetization. No polish.

Question: is leaving camp → finding stuff → environmental chaos → bringing it home actually fun?

Phase 2 — Closed alpha

Track spawn → objective → leave camp → first loot → first danger → objective reached → extraction → first upgrade → second expedition.

Phase 3 — Beta

Get enough real traffic to compare retention, engagement, monetization and benchmark performance. Do not make up universal D1 targets; compare against relevant Roblox cohorts.

Part XI — LiveOps

The content cadence must match team capacity. Never promise “new world every Friday.”

  • Weekly-ish: bug fixes, QoL, small contract, one cosmetic, balance.
  • Every 2–3 weeks: one meaningful reusable module: bug, vehicle component family, event or contract chain.
  • Every 6–8 weeks: major permanent addition: sub-zone, settlement feature, weather system, vehicle category.

Seasonal events

  • Halloween: giant carved pumpkin dungeon, candy drops, fog, special insects.
  • Christmas: snow changes traversal, toy packaging creates salvage, holiday lights become ziplines.
  • Summer: pool content, BBQ, heat, fireworks debris.

Part XII — Technology architecture

Server authority

Server validates currency, loot generation, inventory changes, damage, upgrade costs, purchase entitlements and mission rewards. Client says “I attempted to pick this up,” never “give me 9,999 batteries.”

Save structure

PlayerProfile

 ├─ VehicleParts
 ├─ VehiclePresets
 ├─ Cosmetics
 ├─ Settlement
 ├─ Statistics
 └─ PurchaseEntitlements

Every schema change needs explicit migrations.

Purchase handling

Receipt handling must be idempotent. Server crashes must neither lose a purchase nor grant it repeatedly.

Exploit monitoring

Track impossible pickup rates, impossible currency jumps, teleport velocity, items appearing without provenance, physically impossible contract times and excessive remote calls. Flag anomalies with confidence rather than auto-banning every weird event.

Physics

Not every grass blade has physics. Not every scrap object simulates forever. Use socketed vehicle systems, pooled debris, LOD, streaming, collision groups and strict active-NPC budgets. Dynamic clutter should often look physical without being hundreds of networked rigid bodies.

Mobile first

Core input budget: movement, camera, attack/use, dodge, interact and two equipment slots. No fourteen-hotkey UI.

Aspect-ratio audit

  • 16:9 PC/console
  • 19.5:9 modern phones
  • 20:9 phones
  • 4:3 tablets
  • 21:9 ultrawide

Use anchors/scale rather than hardcoded pixels. Critical HUD stays in safe zones, store UI never blocks mobile movement controls, and controller navigation must work fully on console.

Accessibility

  • Color is never the only rarity signal.
  • Hazards pair color with shape/audio.
  • Camera shake slider.
  • Reduced motion.
  • Separate music/SFX controls.
  • Sensitivity sliders.
  • Toggle/hold sprint options.
  • Potential arachnophobia mode.

Part XIII — Production scope

Ideal small professional core team:

  • 1 gameplay/lead engineer
  • 1 systems/backend engineer
  • 1 environment/3D artist
  • 1 prop/vehicle/character artist
  • 1 animator/VFX generalist
  • 1 UI/UX + 2D person
  • Fractional sound/music, QA, key art, community/marketing

Roughly 5–7 strong core people can make a serious version. A solo developer can prototype it, but should not attempt the full vision at once.

Version 1.0 must ship

  • Shed Camp
  • Patio
  • Garden
  • Sandbox
  • Partial Woodpile
  • One buggy family
  • 15–25 vehicle components
  • ~10 meaningful tools/gadgets
  • 4–6 ordinary enemies
  • 1 predator
  • 6–8 dynamic events
  • ~20 contracts using reusable structures
  • Field Guide
  • Settlement progression
  • Basic trophies
  • Cosmetics
  • Party/co-play
  • Analytics
  • Monetization basics

Version 1.0 explicitly does not ship

House interior, pool boats, street, neighbor yard, PvP, trading, freeform construction, pets, guild warfare, procedural worlds, 40 vehicles, 70 weapons, fishing, farming, cooking skill trees, marriage systems or cryptocurrency.

Part XIV — Self-debate: trying to kill the winner

Attack #1: “This is literally Grounded.”

Attack: Tiny humans, backyard, bugs, crafting.

Defense: If it becomes traditional survival, yes. The identity must be junk vehicles + expeditions + salvage + dynamic disasters + persistent settlement, with light crafting and fast runs.

Verdict: Real risk, manageable through design and art direction.

Attack #2: “Too complicated for Roblox kids.”

The first interaction is pick thing up → attach thing → drive. Deeper optimization appears later. Low floor, high ceiling.

Attack #3: “This will run like shit on phones.”

Probably the biggest technical threat. Giant grass, bugs, vehicles, rain and physics are expensive. This is why freeform construction dies at launch and weak Android testing starts in prototype.

Attack #4: “Players will see every zone and leave.”

Correct if the map is a museum. Dynamic events, collections, settlement progression, contracts and vehicle experimentation must turn the world into a system.

Attack #5: “Vehicles aren't actually fun.”

Then kill or redesign them in week one. No emotional attachment. If the buggy sucks, fix movement before building art.

Attack #6: “Too many progression systems.”

Don't surface them all immediately. First session: scrap. Later: blueprints, Field Guide, settlement mastery.

Attack #7: “How do you monetize survival without ruining it?”

Character, vehicle and base identity create visible surfaces for cosmetics. No need to monetize damage.

Attack #8: “Solo players get screwed.”

Mandatory objectives stay solo-completable. Group play creates chaos and optional coordination, not hard locks.

Attack #9: “Updates will be too expensive.”

Everyday household objects are cheaper content than handcrafted fantasy civilizations. One object can be prop, resource, objective, vehicle component, trophy and event object.

Attack #10: “Copycats will clone it.”

They will. The moat is not secrecy; it is production speed, systems depth, community, data and polish.

Attack #11: “What if Roblox stops recommending us?”

Use Home, Search, social creators/clips and returning-player community systems. Never depend on a single spike.

Attack #12: “Maybe it simply isn't fun.”

This is the most important objection, and no document can answer it.

Can four people driving a shitty bottle-cap buggy through a gigantic garden while a sprinkler tries to kill them make them laugh?

If yes, we may have something. If no, kill it.

The analytics dashboard

Watch Home play-through, <60s bounce, 61–180s bounce, first-session survival, average session, D1, D7, D30, play days/user, co-play, contract funnel, first upgrade, second expedition, payer conversion, ARPPU, store conversion, revenue by product, device retention and acquisition-source retention.

Single most important custom metric

Second Expedition Rate

Percentage of first-time players who begin another expedition after completing or failing the first.

If 100 people finish the first mission and 8 start another, the game is fucked. If 65 immediately turn around and go back outside, pay attention.

Second-most important custom metric

Show-Off Interaction Rate

How many returning players interact with garage customization, trophy rooms, settlement cosmetics and other players' vehicles. Identity is what turns months into years.

Final design commandments

  1. Fun before content.
  2. The player leaves camp within one minute.
  3. Nothing exists solely to stretch playtime.
  4. Every update should interact with multiple systems.
  5. Players buy things because they care, not because they're annoyed.
  6. The world should create stories players didn't expect.
  7. Mobile performance is a design constraint, not cleanup.
  8. Don't add another currency unless necessary.
  9. Don't add trading before the economy is bulletproof.
  10. Don't scale advertising until retention deserves traffic.
  11. Never mistake CCU spikes for product-market fit.
  12. If players aren't voluntarily doing a second run, stop adding content and repair the loop.

Final verdict

The strongest Roblox opportunity here is not another meme simulator and not a hyper-serious pseudo-AAA project that forgets why Roblox spreads. It is a game with a ridiculously obvious Roblox-style fantasy on the surface and a legitimately deep game underneath.

3 INCHES TALL does that unusually well.

  • A child sees: “LOL I'M TINY AND THAT'S A HUGE DOG.”
  • A teenager sees: “We can upgrade this dumbass buggy and raid Spider Hollow.”
  • An older player sees: “There's an actual extraction/progression/build strategy here.”
  • A YouTuber sees: lawnmower chase footage.
  • A TikToker sees: 12-second disaster clips.
  • A collector sees: Field Guide.
  • A completionist sees: blueprints.
  • A roleplayer sees: settlement.
  • A spender sees: vehicle/base/character customization.
  • A friend group sees: co-op chaos.
  • The developer sees: a backyard that can continuously generate content without needing another anime IP every month.

Shopkeeper Street slightly beat it on raw theoretical scoring, but 3 Inches Tall was the one I'd actually put development time behind because it combined a high ceiling with much more survivable execution risk.

Do not design for the screenshot where the game has 2 million CCU. Design for the screenshot two years later where it still has 40,000.
